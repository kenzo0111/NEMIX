62: INTRODUCTION
63: Context of the Study
64: Higher education institutions rely on supply and inventory management to monitor the receipt, distribution, and use of office supplies, equipment, and instructional materials. At University of Camarines Norte (UCN), these activities are handled by the Supply and Property Management Office (SPMO). As the volume of records grows, paper-based procedures can make inventory monitoring and report preparation more difficult. The researchers therefore seek to examine the SPMO’s existing procedures and identify where a digital system could support its work.
65: Discrepancies between physical inventory and recorded balances can affect operations and decision-making. Shahrabi (2023) examined transaction errors that cause such discrepancies and compared periodic manual counting with RFID-based tracking. The study found that RFID could reduce inventory record errors in the model examined, while also recognizing tracking failures and implementation costs. These findings suggest that RFID may assist inventory checking, provided that its performance is tested in the setting where it will be used.
66: Baylosis et al. (2023) developed a web-based inventory management system for a small business in Pagadian City, Philippines. Their system replaced paper-based processes with electronic inventory records and automated reports. The authors reported improvements in recording and processing inventory information. Although that system served as a business rather than a higher education institution, it demonstrates how a web-based platform can bring inventory transactions and reports into one system.
67: Manual inventory procedures may contribute to wasted resources, shortages, and difficulty monitoring equipment (Madu & Izuchukwu, 2022). Nasution (2022) discussed the use of digital records to improve inventory processes. Unhelkar et al. (2022) further described how RFID can increase visibility of items and provide data for supply-chain decision support. Together, these studies provide a basis for considering digital records and RFID in the proposed UCN system. Their reported benefits, however, must be evaluated against the SPMO’s actual workflow and requirements.
68: Tan and Sidhu (2022) reviewed RFID and Internet of Things applications in supply-chain management. They discussed how connected identification devices can provide inventory information and support decisions across different stages of a supply chain. Dhingra et al. (2024) also described the use of RFID for identifying and monitoring tagged items. These studies support the proposed use of RFID for eligible UCN inventory items. A tag scan identifies an item, but the proposed system must still connect that scan to the correct inventory record and transaction.
69: The legal setting also supports examination of an inventory system. Section 19 of the New Government Procurement Act, Republic Act No. 12009, permits procuring entities to adopt warehousing and inventory systems, subject to applicable budgeting, accounting, and auditing rules. Manuel (2025) and Planergy (2025) discuss digital approaches to procurement and resource management. These sources provide context for modernization, but they do not establish that the proposed UCN system or its reports already complies with government requirements. The researchers will check generated reports against the forms and procedures applicable to UCN.
70: To address the inventory challenges identified by SPMO, the researchers propose a web-based Smart Supply and Inventory Management System designed to record transactions, identify eligible RFID-tagged items, monitor stock records, and generate reports. The study will verify whether these functions operate as intended and assess users’ evaluations of the system’s functional suitability, usability, and performance efficiency. Findings will establish the system’s demonstrated capabilities, identify operational limitations, and provide a basis for recommending improvements to existing inventory management processes within SPMO.
71: Research Objectives
72:  The primary objective of this study is to design, develop, and evaluate a web-based Smart Supply and Inventory Management System for the Supply and Property Management Office (SPMO) of University of Camarines Norte. The proposed system will integrate RFID identification, inventory transaction recording, stock monitoring, and automated report generation to support the office’s daily operations. The study will test these functions and assess the system’s acceptance among its intended users, with the aim of improving inventory record management and supporting the preparation of reports based on applicable institutional and government requirements.
73: Specifically, this study aims to:
74: 1. Document the existing procedures for receiving, issuing, counting, and reporting supplies and property, including the difficulties experienced by SPMO personnel;
75: 2. Determine the system requirements; develop functions for inventory transactions, RFID identification of eligible tagged items, stock monitoring, transaction history, and generation of the applicable COA Compliant (RIS, RSMI, RPCI, and Stock Card reports); and verify these functions through documented tests and comparison of generated reports with their source transactions.
76: 3. Determine the level of acceptance of the system among UCN-SPMO staff using the ISO/IEC 25010 quality standards in terms of:
77: a) Functional suitability;
78: b) Usability; and
79: c) Performance efficiency.

89: Scope and Delimitation
90: This study focuses on the design, development, and evaluation of a web-based Smart Supply and Inventory Management System for the Supply and Property Management Office (SPMO) of University of Camarines Norte (UCN). Authorized SPMO personnel and Supply Coordinators will access the system through assigned user accounts. The system will support the recording of received and issued items, monitoring of stock balances, review of transaction histories, and preparation of inventory reports. These functions are intended to help personnel retrieve records and carry out routine inventory activities.
91: The system will use passive RFID technology to identify inventory items assigned compatible tags. The SPMO and researchers will determine which supplies and property are suitable for tagging. For consumable supplies, the recorded quantity will be updated through receipt, issuance, and physical-count transactions; an RFID scan alone will not determine the quantity remaining. Items that cannot be read reliably will be identified through a documented manual procedure. The system will also generate RIS, RSMI, RPCI, and Stock Card reports using its stored transaction data. The generated reports will be checked against the forms and procedures applicable to UCN and relevant Commission on Audit guidance.
92: The study covers inventory activities after items have been received by the SPMO. It excludes competitive bidding, supplier selection, payments, financial accounting, payroll, disbursements, and integration with the College Accounting Office. Faculty members and students will not have direct access to the system or a module for borrowing items. RFID identification will operate only within the reader’s effective range; the system will not provide GPS tracking or continuous monitoring of items taken off campus. Long-term hardware maintenance and institution-wide deployment are also outside the scope of this study.
93: The developed system will undergo functional testing and evaluation by its intended users. SPMO personnel and Supply Coordinators will assess its functional suitability, usability, and performance efficiency using criteria adapted from ISO/IEC 25010. External IT professionals will conduct separate technical evaluations. The findings will describe the system’s tested capabilities and limitations, including any differences between generated reports and the forms required for UCN’s inventory procedures.
94: This study was conducted from the Second Semester of School Year 2025–2026 through the First Semester of School Year 2026–2027. The research commenced during the Second Semester of School Year 2025–2026 with the identification of the existing inventory procedures and problems encountered by the Supply and Property Management Office (SPMO), requirements gathering, planning, and initial system design. The study continued through the First Semester of School Year 2026–2027, covering the development, refinement, integration, testing, and evaluation of the proposed system. Thus, the findings and system implementation presented in this study are limited to the conditions, requirements, processes, and data gathered within this academic period.
95: Within these delimitations, the proposed system is intended to improve how the UCN-SPMO records, monitors, and reports inventory. Its focus on core administrative tasks allows the study to evaluate whether the system addresses the difficulties identified in the office’s current procedures. The findings may also guide future enhancements, such as access for other authorized users or integration with procurement and accounting systems.

107: Inventory Procedures of Supply and Property Management 
108: Inventory Management plays a crucial role in ensuring the proper monitoring, control, and accountability of organizational resources, particularly in offices such as the Supply and Property Management Offices (SPMO). Despite the advancement in technology, many institutions continue to rely on manual and paper-based inventory procedures. While these methods are simple and widely practiced, they present several limitations that affect efficiency, accuracy, and timeliness of operations.
109: One of the most common issues associated with manual inventory systems in UCN SPMO is their susceptibility to human error. Processes that involve handwritten records, manual encoding, and physical counting are prone to mistakes such as miscounts and incorrect data entry. Over time, these errors accumulate and result in discrepancies 
110: between actual inventory and record data. Such inconsistencies affect the reliability of reports and may lead to poor decision-making. As noted by Wynn (2021) frequent errors in manual inventory processes reduce the effectiveness of inventory control and overall management performance. Similarly, Mahzan and Lee (2021) emphasized that human errors during manual counting significantly contribute to inventory inaccuracies.
111: In addition to Accuracy issues, manual inventory procedures often result in slow and inefficient workflows. Routine activities such as recording transactions, verifying stock levels, and preparing reports require multiple steps and physical document handling. These processes consume time and may cause delays, especially when dealing with large volumes of inventory. Omisola et al. (2023) observed that traditional inventory systems commonly lead to inefficiencies, including delays and poor stock control, due to reliance on manual documentation. This is further supported by Janjire et al. (2021), who pointed out that manual tracking and spreadsheet updates are time-consuming and prone to errors, ultimately reducing productivity.
112: Another significant limitation of manual systems is the lack of real-time visibility of inventory data. Since updates are recorded manually and often after transactions occur, there is a delay in reflecting the actual status of inventory. This makes it difficult for personnel to access accurate and up-to-date information when needed. According to Tan et al. (2025), delayed updates and inaccurate data in traditional inventory systems significantly reduce operational efficiency. In the SPMO, this limitation can affect the timely distribution of supplies and hinder effective planning and resource allocation.
113: The continued use of logbooks and spreadsheet applications also contributes to inefficiencies in inventory management. While these tools are accessible, they are not designed to support complex inventory operations. Retrieving data can be slow, and there is higher risk of data loss or duplication. Rakhman et al. (2025) noted that inventory management systems relying on manual encoding and spreadsheet tools may often experience delays in data processing and retrieval. Similarly, the study of Lius and Arisandi (2025) found that manual recording methods frequently result in errors, data loss, and ineffective stock monitoring.
114: Institutional studies further highlight the challenges of manual inventory systems. In organizational settings, manual procedures have been found to limit efficiency in tracking and managing resources. Ayochok and Samudio Perez (2024) reported that manual processes and outdated tools hinder effective resource management, while Fajar et al. (2025) observed that manual inventory practices in government offices lead to delays, inaccurate records, and reduced work efficiency. De Arco (2024) emphasized that manual checking of stock availability increases the time required to locate items and reduces inventory visibility.
115: Furthermore, manual inventory systems are limited in their ability to adapt to modern operational demands. As organizations handle increasing volumes of transactions, the need for faster and more reliable systems becomes more evident. Traditional methods lack the flexibility and scalability required to manage complex inventory processes effectively. Kittisak (2023) identified inefficient inventory control systems as a major challenge, while Nweje and Taiwo (2025) also explained that conventional approaches struggle to cope with the complexity of modern supply chain operations.
116: Collectively, the reviewed literature provides a foundational framework for the first objective of this study, which seeks to evaluate the current manual inventory procedures and identify the specific operational challenges encountered by the UCN SPMO. The cited studies uniformly establish that traditional, paper-based, and spreadsheet-reliant systems are inherently vulnerable to human error, operational delays, and a critical lack of real-time visibility. By aligning these broader academic findings with the local context of the SPMO, this review validates the necessity of systematically assessing the office's existing challenges. Furthermore, these literatures serve as a crucial comparative baseline, demonstrating that the inefficiencies currently experienced by the institution are not isolated incidents, but rather part of a well-documented systemic flaw in manual inventory management. Ultimately, this justifies the critical need to thoroughly baseline the current system's limitations before proposing a modernized, technological intervention.
117: RFID-Based Inventory System and Automated Reports
118: The development of a Smart Supply and Inventory Management System that incorporates RFID technology and automated reports mechanisms reflects the ongoing transition from traditional inventory practices to intelligent and data-driven systems. Existing literature consistently emphasizes that modern inventory environments require not only accurate tracking of items but also real-time monitoring, automated documentation, and compliance with regulatory standards. These requirements are particularly relevant in institutional settings where transparency, accountability, and reporting accuracy are critical.
119: RFID technology plays a crucial role in enabling real-time inventory tracking. Unlike manual or barcode-based systems, RFID allows automatic identification and monitoring of items without the need for direct line of sight scanning. Chen et al. (2022) demonstrated that integrating RFID with cloud databases and user applications significantly reduces human error and labor costs, as data is captured automatically rather than manually encoded. This capability is further supported by Soltanirad et al. (2022), who explained that RFID systems using wireless networks enable continuous monitoring of inventory across storage areas, making them suitable for dynamic environments such as warehouses. In a similar context, Chokshi et al (2022) showed that RFID- based systems can track equipment location and usage in real time, ensuring that items are properly accounted for and authenticated throughout their lifecycle.
120: The operational benefits of RFID extend beyond tracking and into overall efficiency and performance. Ton et al. (2024) conducted a comparative analysis between RFID and barcode systems and found that RFID significantly reduced inventory levels and operational costs while slightly improving customer satisfaction. This suggests that RFID not only enhances internal processes but also contributes to better service delivery. Crooks and Haddud (2025) further explained that organizations adopting RFID technology experience improvements in reliability and logistics efficiency, particularly in distribution and warehouse operations. However, their study also highlighted practical challenges such as high implementation costs and limited technical knowledge, indicating that while RFID is beneficial, careful planning is necessary for successful adoption. Supporting this, Panigrahi et al. (2024) found that RFID contributes to improved operational performance and decision-making, particularly in environments where inventory accuracy and efficiency are essential.
121: The integration of RFID with IoT technologies enhances the capabilities of inventory systems by enabling continuous data exchange and system connectivity. Rani et al. (2024) emphasized that Iot-based inventory systems, which combine RFID tags and sensors, provide real-time visibility and improve transparency in operations. This integration allows organizations to monitor inventory conditions, movements, and usage without manual intervention. Mashayekhy et al. (2022) further explained that IoT addresses the limitations of traditional inventory systems by enabling automated data collection and improving coordination between different system components. In practical applications, Goha and Geea (2025) demonstrated how an Iot driven RFID system can monitor asset status while generating automated reports and compliance indicators. Their study highlights the importance of integrating monitoring and reporting functions within a single system to ensure both operational efficiency and adherence to standards.
122: One of the critical aspects of inventory management in government institutions is compliance with standardized reporting formats such as the Report on Supplies and Materials Issued (RSMI), Report on Physical Count of Inventories (RPCI), Requisition and Issue Slip (RIS), and Stock Cards. The study conducted on secondary school supply offices in Sorsogon (2025) highlighted that these reports are essential for documenting inventory movement and ensuring accountability. However, the manual preparation of such reports places a significant administrative burden on supply officers. Automating these reporting processes within a digital system not only reduces workload but also ensures the accuracy and timeliness of submitted reports.
123: Similarly, the study by Gacosta and De Castro (2025) emphasized the importance of digitizing government prescribed forms, including RIS and other inventory documents, to improve consistency and accountability across departments. By embedding these forms within a system, organizations can ensure that all transactions are properly recorded and aligned with auditing standards. This is further supported by Tingson and Pomperada (2025), who explained that modern property management systems are designed with built-in modules capable of generating reports that comply with Commission on Audit (COA) requirements, thereby enhancing transparency and audit readiness.
124: The role of automation is further strengthened by the integration of intelligent algorithms with inventory systems. Suya et al. (2026) demonstrated that combining descriptive analytics with rule-based algorithms allows systems to automate essential functions such as tracking, monitoring, and report generation. This eliminates the need for repetitive manual tasks and ensures that required reports are generated efficiently. Such capabilities are essential in institutional environments where timely and accurate reporting is necessary for operational and compliance purposes.
125: The push for modernization in inventory and procurement systems is also reinforced by legal frameworks such as the Republic Act. No. 12009. The law encourages government agencies to adopt modern warehousing and inventory systems, including cloud-based solutions, to improve the tracking and management of procured goods. According to the Official Gazette (2024), this initiative supports more efficient planning and monitoring of inventory. Orencia (2025) also emphasized that RA 12009 promotes the use of digital tools to enhance transparency and accountability in procurement processes, while Cabayacruz et al. (2025) highlighted the role of digital platforms in improving operational efficiency and accessibility of procurement related information. These mandates provide a strong legal foundation for the development of automated and technology-driven inventory systems.
126: In terms of tracking technologies, RFID has been widely recognized as an effective solution for improving inventory accuracy and efficiency. Research published in the International Journal on Informatics Visualization (2022) confirmed that RFID significantly enhances real-time monitoring and overall effectiveness of inventory control. This technological integration is further supported by Aguila and Balba (2025), who found that RFID-based systems allow instantaneous updates of inventory records, thereby streamlining operational workflows.
127: In addition to compliance, automated and RFID based systems improve transparency and accountability in inventory management. Bandara et al. (2024) demonstrated that RFID systems integrated with databases and user interfaces allow easy access to historical inventory data, enabling better tracking and auditing of resources. Similarly, Da Souza et al. (2024) provided evidence that RFID implementation significantly reduces inventory loss and improves monitoring through real-time dashboards. Their study, conducted in a high-stakes environment such as a blood bank, highlights how accurate tracking and automated monitoring can reduce error and improve operational outcomes.
128: Collectively, the reviewed literature provides the theoretical and technological foundation for the second objective of this study, which aims to develop an RFID-based inventory management system integrated with automated reporting mechanisms. The cited studies validate that transitioning from manual tracking to an RFID and IoT-driven framework directly resolves the operational challenges of traditional systems by enabling real-time visibility, reducing human error, and improving overall efficiency. Furthermore, the extensive literature on digital compliance—specifically the automation of government-mandated documents like the RSMI, RPCI, and RIS, alongside adherence to RA 12009 directly justifies the study’s goal of creating a system that goes beyond mere tracking. Ultimately, these related studies establish that the proposed technological intervention is not only functionally viable but practically essential for ensuring transparency, accountability, and strict compliance with institutional and state auditing standards.
129: System Acceptance Based on ISO/IEC 25010 Quality Standards
130: The evaluation of system acceptance is a critical phase in the development of any information system, particularly in institutional environments where usability, reliability, and efficiency directly affect daily operations. The ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model has been widely adopted as a standard framework for assessing system quality and user acceptance. Rojas et al. (2025) emphasized that ISO/IEC 25010 has gained global recognition as a comprehensive model for evaluating software deployed in public administration and organizational contexts. Its structured approach allows researchers to assess whether a system meets both technical and user-centered requirements, making it suitable for evaluating newly developed inventory systems in offices such as the UCN-SPMO.
131: The application of ISO/IEC 25010 in system evaluation is not limited to a single domain but extends across various types of information systems. Abana and Sy (2022) demonstrated that the framework can be applied even in specialized systems such as machine vision applications, highlighting its flexibility in assessing both hardware integrated and software-based solutions. Similarly, Devaldi Akbar Suryadi and Sulistiyani (2022) utilized the ISO 25010 model to evaluate information quality in an application system, showing that the framework enables systematic identification of strengths and weaknesses within a system. These studies suggest that ISO 25010 provides a consistent and reliable basis for evaluating system quality regardless of the application domain.
132: In the context of inventory and property management systems, adherence to ISO 25010 standards has become a common evaluative practice. Jesalva et al. (2024) evaluated a web-based inventory system using ISO 25010 criteria, focusing on functionality, usability, compatibility, and performance efficiency. Their study illustrates that the framework is particularly relevant for systems handling structured data and operational workflows. Similarly, Lestari et al. (2024) conducted usability testing based on ISO 25010 and reported a high usability score, indicating that the system was feasible for daily operational use. These findings reinforce the importance of usability as a key determinant of whether a system will be accepted by its intended users.
133: Functional suitability, referred to in this study as functional stability, is one of the core dimensions in ISO 25010 used to evaluate whether a system performs its intended functions accurately and consistently. Sarwosri et al. (2023) explained that functional suitability is essential in measuring software quality, as it determines whether system features align with user requirements. Nuzula and Rochimah (2023) further emphasized that evaluating functionality alongside usability allows researchers to identify both the strengths and limitations of a system from the user’s perspective. Assifa et al. (2023) added that continuous functional evaluation supports system improvement and ensures that users can effectively interact with the system without encountering operational issues.
134: Usability is another critical factor influencing user acceptance. Systems that are easy to learn, understand, and operate are more likely to be adopted by users. Gacosta and De Castro (2025) observed that staff members showed strong agreement in terms of usability when evaluating a digitized supply and property management system, suggesting that ease of use directly contributes to acceptance. Encaranacion et al. (2025) similarly reported high user satisfaction scores when systems were evaluated using ISO 25010 standards, indicating that usability plays a significant role in shaping positive user perceptions. Mission and Rubio (2024) also found that systems achieving high rating in functional suitability and usability were considered acceptable to highly acceptable, reinforcing the idea that usability is closely tied to user acceptance in organizational settings.
135: Performance efficiency, which refers to the systems responsiveness, speed, and resource utilization, is equally important in determining system acceptance. The study on the Mobile JKN application (2024) found that performance efficiency had the strongest influence on perceived system effectiveness, indicating that users prioritize fast and responsive systems. Yuniasri et al. (2024) further explained that performance efficiency can be evaluated through time behaviour, resource utilization, and system capacity, providing a detailed basis for assessing how well a system performs under operational conditions.
136: The relationship between ISO 25010 evaluation results and user acceptance is further supported by empirical studies. Aguila and Balba (2025) reported that an RFID-based system achieved a 100% success rate in functionality and user satisfaction when evaluated using ISO 25010 metrics. This demonstrates that rigorous testing based on standardized criteria can ensure that the system meets both technical and user expectations. Similarly, Sutisna and Rahman (2025) found that systems evaluated under ISO 25010 achieved high scores in functionality and reliability, indicating that the framework effectively validates whether a system can handle complex operational processes.
137: Collectively, the reviewed literature establishes a robust methodological foundation for the third objective of this study, which seeks to determine the level of end-user satisfaction and system acceptance of the newly developed RFID-based system among SPMO personnel. By anchoring the evaluation on the internationally recognized ISO/IEC 25010 framework, this study aligns with proven academic and industry practices for assessing digital interventions in institutional settings. The cited studies validate that measuring specific dimensions—such as functional stability, usability, and performance efficiency—is essential not only for verifying technical accuracy but also for ensuring successful adoption by end-users. Ultimately, these related works justify the use of the ISO 25010 model as the primary mechanism to prove that the proposed Smart Supply and Inventory Management System for the UCN SPMO is functionally viable, reliable, and highly acceptable for daily operational use.
138: Synthesis of the Art 
139: The body of existing literature demonstrates how modern technologies significantly improve traditional inventory management practices, particularly in institutional environments such as Supply and Property Management Offices (SPMO). The proposed Smart Supply and Inventory Management System: An RFID Inventory Tracking and Automated Reports represents a technological advancement aimed at transforming manual, paper-based inventory processes into an efficient, automated, and data-driven system. This innovation is expected to enhance operational efficiency, accuracy, transparency, and compliance with government standards.
140: Several similarities were identified among the reviewed studies. The works of Wynn (2021), Linuwih and Handayati (2025), and Upadhyaya (2024) consistently emphasize that inventory management plays a crucial role in organizational performance, yet manual processes often lead to inaccuracies and inefficiencies. These studies collectively highlight that errors in inventory records negatively affect decision-making, financial outcomes, and operational effectiveness. Similarly, Omisola et al. (2023), Whaley and White (2024), and Tan et al. (2025) agreed that traditional inventory systems, which rely heavily on manual documentation and delayed data updates, are prone to inefficiencies such as stock discrepancies, delays, and reduced productivity.
141: In addition, several studies focused on the challenges experienced in organizations still relying on manual and semi digital tools. Rakhman et al. (2025), Fajar et al. (2025), and Lius and Arisandi (2025) pointed out that inventory systems dependent on logbooks and spreadsheet applications often suffer from slow data processing, retrieval delays, and high susceptibility to errors. Institutional-based studies, such as those conducted by Ayochok and Perez (2024) and De Arco (2024), further emphasized that manual procedures limit visibility and efficiency in managing resources. 
142: Moreover, the reviewed literature shows strong agreement on the effectiveness of RFID and Iot technologies in modernizing inventory systems. Studies by Chen et al. (2022), Soltanirad et al. (2022), and Chokshi et al. (2022) demonstrated that RFID-based systems enable real-time tracking, reduce human intervention, and improve inventory accuracy. Similarly, Ton et al. (2024) and Crooks and Haddud (2025) found that RFID adoption leads to improved operational efficiency, reduced costs, and enhanced logistics performance. The integration of Iot technologies, as discussed by Rani et al. (2024) and Mashayekhy et al. (2022), further strengthens inventory systems by enabling continuous monitoring and automated data collection. These technological advancements are directly aligned with the present study’s objective of developing an RFID-based inventory tracking system.
143: Another important similarity among the studies is the emphasis on automation and reporting. Research by Gumilao (2024), Tingson and Pomperada (2025), and Gacosta and De Castro (2025) highlighted the importance of automated report generation in ensuring accuracy, consistency, and compliance with organizational standards. The automated generation of reports such as RSMI, RPCI, RIS, and Stock Cards reduces administrative workload and enhances accountability. Furthermore, the integration of analytics and rule-based systems, as presented by Suya et al. (2026), demonstrates how automation can streamline inventory processes and improve overall system efficiency.
144: In terms of governance and compliance, several studies emphasized the role of policies and legal frameworks in encouraging system modernization. The implementation of Republic Act No. 12009 promotes the adoption of digital and cloud-based inventory systems to improve transparency, efficiency, and accountability in government operations. Supporting studies by Orencia (2025) and Cabayacruz et al. (2025) highlighted that digital platforms enhance procurement transparency and operational efficiency.
145: Furthermore, the evaluation of system acceptance using ISO/IEC 25010 standards is widely supported in the literature. Rojas et al. (2025) emphasized that ISO 25010 is a globally recognized framework for assessing system quality, particularly in public sector applications. Studies by Jesalva et al. (2024) and Lestari et al. (2024) demonstrated that usability, functionality, and performance efficiency are critical factors in determining system acceptance. Similarly, Gacosta and De Castro (2025) and Encaranacion et al. (2025) found that systems with high usability and reliable performance are more likely to be accepted by users. These findings validate the use of ISO 25010 in evaluating the developed system in terms of functional stability, usability, and performance efficiency.
146: Despite the similarities, several gaps were identified in the reviewed literature. Most existing studies focus either on manual inventory challenges or on standalone RFID and automated systems, but few integrate both aspects into a single, comprehensive solution tailored for institutional supply offices. Additionally, while many systems provide inventory tracking, not all incorporate automated generation of COA-compliant reports required in government settings. Some studies also emphasize either software development or hardware implementation but lack full integration between RFID technology and reporting systems within a unified platform.
147: The present study addresses these gaps by developing a fully integrated system that combines RFID-based tracking with automated report generation specifically designed for the UCN-SPMO. Unlike previous studies that focus on general inventory systems, this research considers the unique operational requirements, workflows, and compliance standards of a government supply office. By integrating real-time tracking, automated documentation, and ISO-based system evaluation, the study provides a more comprehensive and practical solution.

166: METHODOLOGY
167:  This chapter presents the research methodology used by the researchers in their study entitled Smart Supply and Inventory Management System: An RFID Inventory Tracking and Automated Reports. It discusses Software Development Methodology, Hardware Development, Schedule of Activities, Source of Data, Population of the Study, Data Collection Method, Data Analysis, and Ethical Considerations in the development of the study.
168: Research Design 
169:  The research design for this study utilizes a developmental and descriptive-evaluative approach that integrates software engineering methodology to address the inventory management needs of the Supply and Property Management Office (SPMO). The developmental component covers requirements gathering, system design, implementation, and testing of the web application and RFID component using Agile Scrum. The descriptive-evaluative component examines existing inventory procedures and assesses the developed system through documented tests and feedback from intended users.
170: Software Development Methodology
171:  This section discusses the software development methodology utilized to develop the Smart Supply and Inventory Management System: RFID Inventory Tracking and Automated Reports for Government Compliance. The researchers utilized the Agile Scrum model to oversee the project management and development of the system. This model ensures that the development goes through regulated, iterative cycles. Agile Scrum is a widely adopted iterative and incremental framework in software development, focusing 
172: on continuous communication, collaboration, and adaptability. It aims to deliver high-quality, functional software that meets users’ needs through short development cycles called Sprints. Its flexibility makes it highly suitable for managing the complexities of integrating hardware sensors with web applications. According to Guerrero-Ulloa, Rodríguez-Domínguez, and Hornos (2023), Agile methodologies provide structured task allocation and guidelines for development teams to enhance efficiency and maintain controlled progress when developing Internet of Things (IoT)-based systems, as shown in Figure 1.
174: Figure 1. The Agile Scrum Model
175: The researchers used the Agile Scrum model because of its emphasis on adaptability and continuous stakeholder feedback, which prioritized refining system functionalities—a vital aspect of this study. Additionally, Scrum helps manage unexpected development challenges, allows for early detection of hardware-software integration issues, and reduces resource waste, which is particularly beneficial when working with RFID components. Most importantly, due to the complex requirements of automated government compliance reporting, the researchers required Scrum’s iterative approach to enable them to revisit and refine features at the end of every Sprint until they reached the desired results.
176: According to standard Scrum practices, the development encompasses four key iterative phases: Product Backlog Creation, Sprint Planning, Sprint Execution, and Sprint Review & Retrospective.
177: The Product Backlog Creation Phase centers on grasping the project's overall business requirements and creating a prioritized list of features. In this stage, the project scope is established, system objectives are defined, and user stories are formulated. In this phase, the researchers concentrated on several essential tasks: securing stakeholder consensus on the objectives of the procurement system and identifying the problems encountered in the existing manual inventory process. They conducted thorough interviews with concerned personnel, established a timeline with significant milestones, and compiled all necessary features—such as RFID scanning and compliance reporting—into a master priority list known as the Product Backlog.
178: The Sprint Planning Phase, which occurs at the start of every iteration, is crucial for selecting which features to build next. It involves designing the system architecture and exploring component specifications to be used in enhancing the system's overall functionality for that specific cycle. In this phase, the researchers concentrated on comprehending the selected project requirements and mapping out the technical blueprint. They worked together to make decisions regarding the database structure, user interface design, and the specific programming languages and RFID hardware to be used. Flowcharts and system diagrams were created to provide a clear structure before coding began.
179: The Sprint Execution Phase is the active stage of the methodology. The focus of this stage is to develop the system and gradually construct a comprehensive, functioning system increment that is ready to be tested within a set timebox (usually 1 to 4 weeks). In this phase, the researchers were ready to develop and integrate all components and features for the current Sprint. They focused on coding the web-based interface, implementing the automated report generation, and integrating the IoT RFID tracking modules. Their responsibilities included programming, configuring the hardware logic, and conducting continuous internal testing to ensure the components communicated properly.
180: The Sprint Review and Retrospective Phase is the final stage of a Sprint cycle, signifying the evaluation of the newly built increment. This phase includes presenting the working software to stakeholders, collecting feedback, and reflecting on the team's workflow to improve the next Sprint. In this phase, the researchers collected feedback and insights from potential users by conducting user testing on the completed inventory features. This feedback was then directly utilized to implement necessary updates, fix bugs, or adjust the Product Backlog for the next iteration. Through these continuous cycles of testing and finalization, the system was gradually refined until it was considered fully ready and met all requirements for deployment.
181: Hardware Development 
182: This section details the physical construction and electrical integration of the handheld UHF RFID scanner project. The hardware architecture relies on standard, off-the-shelf components to deliver a compact, ergonomic, and highly efficient device for inventory management, as shown in the hardware overview layout of Figure 2 and the electrical schematics of Figure 3.
183: The core processing and control unit driving the device is a 30-pin ESP32 Development Board. As illustrated in the board connection schematic of Figure 2, this microcontroller serves as the primary integration block, coordinating user inputs and data flow. It is wired to detect manual trigger pulls via a mechanical switch connected to the GPIO33 pin, which operates on an active LOW logic state. The ESP32 utilizes its UART interface to establish reliable, two-way communication with the RFID module, allowing it to parse incoming tag data efficiently.
184: The primary scanning operations are executed by a YRM100 UHF RFID Module, which measures exactly 50 x 50 mm, as shown in the component dimensions of Figure 2. This component is responsible for generating the RF signals necessary to read external tags, feeding the acquired data back to the ESP32 over the established UART connection. To guarantee optimal reading ranges and prevent signal interference, the physical layout explicitly requires the front antenna area to be kept completely free of metal or thick materials, recommending the use of an RF-transparent window made of ABS or PC, as noted in both Figure 2 and Figure 3.
185: To ensure full mobility and sustained operation, the system is powered by a single flat-top 18650 Lithium-ion Battery possessing a nominal voltage of 3.7V. Because the microcontroller and RFID modules require a stable, higher operating voltage, the battery is routed through a USB-C Charge Module equipped with an XL6019 DC-DC Boost Converter, as depicted in the schematic system integration in Figure 3. This module boosts the voltage to provide a stable 5V, 2A power supply to the rest of the system while simultaneously enabling convenient USB Type-C recharging.
186: The entire internal layout is housed within an ergonomic, pistol-grip upper and lower shell designed for 3D printing, as shown in the exploded assembly view of Figure 2. The recommended fabrication materials include ABS, PLA, or PETG, utilizing M3 x 12mm screws and heat-set brass inserts to firmly secure all internal modules and wiring.
188: Figure 2. Hardware Overview and Internal Layout
189: In summary, the hardware schematic and component layout, as comprehensively outlined in Figure 2 and Figure 3, highlight a highly integrated system. By combining the ESP32's processing capabilities, the YRM100's robust scanning range, and a reliable 18650-powered step-up circuit, the resulting device provides a comfortable, untethered, and reliable solution for rapid inventory auditing.

192: Schedule of Activities
193:  In development of the study, researchers used Gantt Chart to illustrate the order of the activities and track the overall progress of the project. This approach is utilized to present the key milestones—such as requirements gathering, system design, development, and user testing–ensuring that every step of the Smart Supply and Inventory Management System remains on schedule.
195: Figure 4. Schedule of Activities
196: The project began with the Sprint Planning phase, which included stakeholder interviews, gathering of system requirements, and the creation of the product backlog. These activities were conducted during the early months of the schedule to establish a strong foundation for the system by identifying user needs and defining the project scope. This phase ensured that all necessary requirements were documented and prioritized before development began.
197: Following this, the Sprint Setup phase focused on selecting user stories from the backlog, designing the web user interface, defining system features, and specifying the RFID hardware requirements. Conducted from February to May, this phase emphasized planning and design, ensuring that both the software and hardware components were well-prepared for implementation. The researchers carefully analyzed system functionalities and technical requirements to align with project objectives.
198: The Sprint Execution phase covered the core development activities, including web development, system iteration, and RFID module integration. Spanning several months, this phase reflects the iterative nature of iterations allowed the researchers to improve features progressively while integrating the RFID technology into the system.
199: Subsequently, the Sprint Review & Retrospective phase involved user testing and the collection of feedback and validation from stakeholders. This phase ensured that the system met the required standards in terms of functionality, usability, and performance. Feedback gathered during this stage was essential for identifying issues and implementing necessary improvements before final deployment.
200: Lastly, the Deployment phase marked the completion of the project, where the system was fully implemented. This final stage ensured that all components were functional and ready for actual use. Overall, the schedule demonstrates a structured yet flexible approach, highlighting how Agile Scrum supports continuous collaboration, iterative progress, and timely completion of the Smart Supply and Inventory Management System.

224: Data Collection Method
225: The data collection for this study will employ a mixed-methods approach using qualitative and quantitative methods. To answer the first research question, the researchers will use qualitative methods: semi-structured interviews with selected Supply and Property Management Office (SPMO) personnel and document analysis of available logbooks, inventory forms, and spreadsheets. These sources will help document existing procedures for receiving, issuing, counting, and reporting items and identify difficulties in the current workflow.
226: To answer the second research question, the researchers will conduct follow-up interviews and requirements-gathering sessions with SPMO personnel and Supply Coordinators. They will review the RIS, RSMI, RPCI, and Stock Card forms used by UCN to identify the information needed for each report. After developing the system, the researchers will conduct documented tests of RFID identification, inventory transactions, stock balances, user access, and report generation. They will compare generated report entries with their source transactions and the applicable forms, recording the expected and actual results of each test.
227: To answer the third research question, the researchers will use quantitative data from a five-point evaluation questionnaire. The SPMO staff member and Supply Coordinators will perform representative inventory tasks before evaluating the system. Five external IT professionals will also evaluate it. The questionnaire will assess functional suitability, usability, and performance efficiency using criteria adapted from ISO/IEC 25010. The ratings of intended users and IT professionals will be reported separately. Respondents will also be invited to describe any problems they encounter while using the system.
228: Data Analysis
229: The researchers will analyze interview responses and inventory documents to identify recurring difficulties in the SPMO’s procedures for receiving, issuing, counting, and reporting items. They will group the findings by workflow stage to describe the current process. To assess the developed system’s functions, they will compare the actual results of documented tests with the expected results and record any failed tests or unresolved issues.
230: The researchers will evaluate the level of acceptance of the Smart Supply and Inventory Management System using a structured questionnaire. After using the system, SPMO personnel, Supply Coordinators, and IT professionals will rate statements on functional suitability, usability, and performance efficiency using a five-point Likert scale. The statements will be based on the selected ISO/IEC 25010 criteria. The ratings will describe respondents’ assessments of the system, while the documented tests will provide separate evidence of whether its functions work as expected.
231: The researchers will determine the frequency of responses for each rating and compute the mean rating for every evaluation criterion. Findings from SPMO personnel and Supply Coordinators will be presented separately from evaluations provided by IT professionals to ensure clear comparison. The mean ratings will be interpreted using the rating intervals specified in Table 2. In addition, the researchers will examine respondents’ written feedback to identify recurring concerns, assess suggested enhancements, and determine areas requiring improvement in the developed system.

265: Ethical Consideration
266: In the development of the study, the researchers will strictly adhere to ethical protocols to protect participants and institutional data. All participants involved in interviews and system evaluations will be provided with informed consent, ensuring they understand the study’s purpose and their voluntary role in the process.
267: To maintain security and confidentiality, the system features a dedicated User Module with a secure login gateway, ensuring that only authorized SPMO personnel can access sensitive inventory records. This role-based access control prevents unauthorized entry and ensures that data privacy is maintained throughout the system’s lifecycle. The researchers also commit to data integrity and transparency, ensuring that the automated reports, such as the RPCI and RSMI, accurately reflect physical counts to maintain accountability in the management of public funds as mandated by RA 9184 and RA 12009.
268: Additionally, by coordinating system features with national guidelines for data-driven decision-making and moral resource distribution, the researchers highlight institutional compliance. To prevent any interference with the college's regular operations, the study also makes sure that all hardware development and testing of RFID components within the UCN premises are carried out in a safe and professional manner. By taking these steps, the study maintains the highest standards of academic and professional integrity while bridging the gap between manual processes and contemporary, compliant inventory management.
